How to Study for 3 Finals in 2 Days

Three finals inside 48 hours is a different problem from a heavy week. You can't give each exam its own day, and every hour you spend on one comes out of the other two. This guide covers how to decide which final gets the most time, a block-by-block plan you can copy, what order to study in, and why those hours should go to recall instead of rereading.

Key Takeaways
  • Don't split the 48 hours evenly. Rank the three finals by the score you need and how ready you feel, and give the most time to the one with the most at stake.
  • The block right before each final is a timed practice test for that course, followed by a pass over everything you missed.
  • Touch all three courses on day one, so the second and third finals don't start cold.
  • Spend study blocks answering questions from memory. Rereading feels productive and holds up worse a week later.
  • Keep a stop time each night. You have to perform three times, so a lost night costs you more than once.

Spend the first 20 minutes on triage

Before you open a single set of notes, write down four things for each final: when it is, what it counts for, the score you need, and how ready you feel (lost, shaky or solid). The final exam calculator gives you the score you need from your current grade, the weight of the final and the grade you want.

Here is how that can play out. The numbers come straight from the calculator's formula: needed score = (target - current × (1 - weight)) ÷ weight.

Course B gets the biggest share: it needs a high score, the final carries the most weight, and you don't feel ready. Course A needs almost as high a score, but you already know the material, so it mostly needs a practice test to confirm that. Course C feels shaky, but a 68% keeps your target, so it gets enough time to make sure you clear that bar and no more.

The trap is ranking by fear. The course that scares you most is not always the one where extra hours change your grade. Ranking by the score you need keeps you honest.

The 48-hour block plan

This template assumes Final 1 is on the morning of day two, Final 2 is the morning of day three and Final 3 is that afternoon. Shift the times to fit yours. Blocks are 60 to 90 minutes with a short break between them. Where it says "top course", put the course that won triage.

What order to study in, and when to sleep

Two rules decide the order. First, the final that comes next owns the block right before it. Whatever triage said, the last serious session before an exam belongs to that exam, and it should be a practice test or a hard recall pass, not a first look at the material. Second, triage decides everything else. The spare blocks go to the course with the most at stake, even if its exam is last.

Within a day, put the hardest work where you are sharpest. For most people that means practice tests earlier and lighter recall later. Leave the last few minutes before bed for a quick pass on the next morning's exam, and don't start new material at night.

On sleep: don't plan an all-nighter. You have to sit three exams, so a lost night shows up in the second and third ones as well as the first. Pick a stop time for each night before you start, and keep it even if a block runs short. If the plan only works by cutting sleep, cut the lowest-stakes course's blocks instead.

Use the hours for recall, not rereading

With 48 hours, the biggest gain is in how you spend each block. Rereading notes and highlights feels like progress. Answering questions from memory feels slower and more uncomfortable. The research points the other way from the feeling.

In Roediger and Karpicke (2006), college students read short prose passages. One week later, students who studied a passage once and then took three recall tests remembered 61% of its ideas, against 40% for students who studied it four times. On a test five minutes after studying, restudying looked better, and the students who kept restudying predicted they would remember more. That is the trap: rereading wins in the moment and loses later. Your finals sit a day or two out, between those two test points, so treat this as a reason to lean on recall rather than a promise of a particular score.

In Karpicke and Blunt (2011), retrieval practice also beat building concept maps with the text open: on short-answer questions a week later, the retrieval group scored 0.67 against 0.45. That compares two ways of spending the same time. It doesn't show concept maps are useless, only that with the clock running, recall gave more.

For math and problem-based courses, mix problem types instead of doing a stack of one kind. In a small study by Rohrer and Taylor (2007), with 18 students, mixed practice scored 63% on a test a week later against 20% for blocked practice, even though blocked practice scored higher during the practice itself (89% vs 60%). A mixed practice test feels worse while you do it. That is not a sign it isn't working.

Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) reviewed ten common study techniques. They rated practice testing and distributed practice "high utility", interleaved practice, elaborative interrogation and self-explanation "moderate", and rereading, highlighting and summarization "low". Those ratings describe how broadly the evidence holds up across students, materials and tests, not how big each effect is.

Spacing has a limit when you only have two days. Cepeda and colleagues (2008) found that the best gap between study sessions grows with how long you need to remember the material. That study used trivia facts and a single review session, so it is a direction rather than a rule for finals. Still, it fits how the plan above is built: each course comes up more than once, a few hours apart, instead of in one long block. If any of these courses leads into a sequel next term, plan a review after break rather than counting on what you crammed.

What a recall block looks like

  • Old exams or practice questions, timed. The closest thing to the real exam. Mark every guess as well as every miss.
  • Blank-page recall. Close everything, write out what you know about one topic, then check it against your notes and fix the gaps in a different color.
  • Formula or definition sheet from memory. Write it from scratch, then compare. Whatever you got wrong goes on the miss list.
  • Mixed problem sets. For quantitative courses, shuffle problems from different chapters so you have to pick the method each time.
  • The miss list. Keep one running list per course. The last 20 minutes before each final's night is a pass through that list, nothing else.

If you have more than 2 days

If your finals are spread across a full week, the trade-offs are different: there is room to space reviews out and give each exam its own day. How to study for multiple exams in one week covers that version, and the finals week planner handles up to six finals across three weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I pull an all-nighter if I have 3 finals in 2 days?

No. You have to perform three times, not once, so a lost night shows up in the second and third exams as well as the first. Set a stop time each night and keep it, and use the last few minutes before bed for a short recall pass on the next morning's exam rather than new material.

Which final should I study for first?

The final that comes first owns the block right before it, and that block should be a timed practice test. Triage decides how big each course's other blocks are: the course with the most points at stake and the lowest readiness gets the largest share, even if its exam is last.

How should I split my time between three finals?

Not evenly. For each final, work out the score you need with a final exam calculator and mark how ready you feel. The course that needs a high score and feels shaky gets the most hours. A course where you only need a modest score and feel solid can get one practice test and a short recall pass.

Is it better to reread my notes or do practice questions?

Practice questions. In Roediger and Karpicke (2006), students who took recall tests remembered 61% of a passage a week later, against 40% for students who restudied it, even though restudying looked better on a test five minutes later. Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing high utility and rereading low utility.
