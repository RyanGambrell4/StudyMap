Spaced Repetition: The Science Behind Why It Works (and How to Use It)

Most people think spaced repetition is an Anki feature. It's actually a scheduling principle built on 140 years of memory research. Understanding the science tells you exactly how to implement it, with or without any software.

In the 1880s, German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus spent years doing something no researcher had done before: systematically studying his own memory. He memorized lists of nonsense syllables and measured how much they had faded at various intervals. His 1885 book is still the starting point for research on forgetting and spacing.

What Ebbinghaus Actually Found

Ebbinghaus was his own and only subject. He learned lists of nonsense syllables until he could repeat them, waited, and then measured how much less time it took to relearn them. He called that reduction "savings." Memory, by this measure, did not fade at a steady rate. It dropped quickly at first and then the decline slowed. That shape is what people mean by the forgetting curve.

One figure from his data is worth knowing: after 24 hours, his savings were 33.7%, meaning relearning took about a third less time than learning the list from scratch. He thought even that was a point or two too high. Many websites quote a full table of forgetting percentages attributed to him; we leave those out because we have not verified them against his text.

He also tested spacing directly. In chapter 8 of the book, he reported that "38 repetitions, distributed in a certain way over the three preceding days, had just as favorable an effect as 68 repetitions made on the day just previous." That is the spacing effect in its first recorded form: spreading practice out did the same work with far fewer repetitions.

"38 repetitions, distributed in a certain way over the three preceding days, had just as favorable an effect as 68 repetitions made on the day just previous." (Ebbinghaus, 1885)

One person memorizing nonsense syllables is a narrow base, which is why the modern research below matters more for students.

Quick answer

Spaced repetition means spreading study sessions out over days and weeks instead of cramming, with gaps that grow as you learn the material. Cepeda and colleagues (2006), reviewing 317 experiments on verbal recall, found spaced practice reliably beat massed practice, and that the best gap grows with how long you need to remember.

The Spacing Effect: What Research Actually Shows

The spacing effect is the finding that spreading practice out beats massing it together for long-term retention. The largest review of it is Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer (2006) in Psychological Bulletin. They pooled 839 assessments from 317 experiments in 184 articles, all on verbal recall tasks such as word lists and facts. Spaced practice beat massed practice, and only 12 of 271 comparisons showed no effect or a reversal.

The same review found that the best gap between study sessions grows with the retention interval, meaning how long you need to remember. For memory tested under a minute later, gaps under a minute were best. For memory tested six months or more later, gaps of at least a month were best. Two caveats: 80% of the comparisons used retention intervals under one day, and only 4% used intervals over a month, so the evidence is thinnest for the time scales that matter most for a semester. The review also does not give a single "X% better" figure, so be wary of anyone who quotes one.

Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted and Pashler (2008) tested the long time scales directly. 1,354 adults learned 32 obscure trivia facts online, reviewed them once after a gap of anywhere from 0 to 105 days, and took a final test 7, 35, 70 or 350 days after that review. For every test delay there was a best gap, and the best gap as a share of the delay shrank as the delay grew: in the authors' words, from "about 20 to 40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5 to 10% of a 1-year test delay." Compared with no gap at all, the best gap produced 64% more recall and 26% more recognition.

In plain terms: if your test is a week away, a review one to three days after first learning fits those numbers. If you need the material for a final months away, gaps of weeks fit better. That study used one review session and trivia facts, so treat these figures as a direction, not a formula.

The core insight: Massed practice (studying the same material over and over in one long session) can feel productive and works well for a test minutes later. Spaced practice produces better retention after a delay, and the longer you need the material, the longer the gaps can be. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated distributed practice one of only two high-utility study techniques, alongside practice testing.

Why does spacing work? The explanation is less settled than the effect. One common account is that after some time has passed, retrieving something takes more effort, and that effortful retrieval strengthens memory more than easy retrieval does. This is also why spacing pairs so well with retrieval practice: each spaced review should be a recall attempt, not a re-read.

How to Implement Spaced Repetition: The Practical System

The practical question is how to actually build this into a study routine. There are two main approaches: software-assisted and manual. Both work. Software is more precise; manual systems are more flexible and work well for students who prefer paper.

A Starting Interval Schedule

No study hands you one correct schedule, and the research above says the best gaps depend on when your exam is. The schedule below is a common rule of thumb with gaps that expand, which fits the pattern Cepeda and colleagues found. Shorten it if your exam is close; stretch it if you are preparing for a cumulative final.

1

Day 1: Initial learning

Engage with the material actively. Take notes, try to understand the concepts, and at the end of your session, write down the 5-10 most important things from memory without looking at your notes. This first active recall attempt sets the baseline.

2

Day 2: First review (1 day later)

Before looking at anything, try to write or recite everything you remember from the previous session. Then check your notes and fill in the gaps. Rate each piece of information as easy, medium, or hard based on how readily it came to mind. Hard items need more frequent future reviews.

3

Day 5: Second review (3 days later)

Same process: attempt retrieval first, then verify. Hard items from the previous session get priority. Items you remembered easily the first time should still be reviewed but may require fewer attempts. Note any material that is still fuzzy and mark it for extra attention.

4

Day 12: Third review (1 week later)

By now, items you know well will come to mind with less effort. Items you do not know will be clearly identifiable. Confidently known items can move to a 2-week interval. Struggling items drop back to a 3-day interval.

5

Day 26: Fourth review (2 weeks later)

Items you have recalled correctly at each step can move to longer gaps. Put your fresh attention on new material and on the items you still miss, rather than re-reviewing what you already know well.

6

Monthly: Maintenance reviews

For material you need months from now, such as content for a cumulative final, a short monthly pass through what you know keeps it in rotation. If you forget an item, reset its interval back to 1 day and run it through the cycle again.

Software vs. Manual: Which Should You Use?

Anki is a widely used spaced repetition app, especially among medical students and language learners. After each card you rate your recall, from "Again" (forgot) to "Easy," and Anki uses that rating to decide when the card comes back.

Anki works well for large volumes of factual material. Medical students use it for pharmacology, anatomy, and pathology, where hundreds of facts need to be retained over years, not weeks. The automation makes it practical at scale.

However, Anki has costs. Setting it up correctly requires learning the software. Creating quality cards takes significant time. Many students spend more time making Anki cards than they spend in actual active recall. If card creation is consuming more time than card review, the system is misconfigured.

When to use Anki: High-volume, highly factual subjects (anatomy, pharmacology, foreign language vocabulary, biochemistry pathways) where you need to retain hundreds or thousands of discrete facts over a long period. MCAT and USMLE prep are the canonical use cases.

When a manual system is fine: Smaller-volume material, conceptual courses where deep understanding matters more than fact retrieval, or any situation where the software overhead would consume more time than the automation saves.

Common Spaced Repetition Mistakes to Avoid

Spaced repetition is simple in principle but has several failure modes in practice that cause students to give up on it or use it ineffectively.

Passive card review. The most common mistake. Students flip cards and read the answer on the back without genuinely attempting to retrieve it first. This converts spaced repetition into spaced re-reading, which throws away the retrieval part of the method. The retrieval attempt must come first, even if you're not sure of the answer.

Cards that are too broad. A flashcard that says "Explain the entire immune system" cannot be effectively retrieved and rated. Cards should test one specific, retrievable fact or concept. "What does a B cell produce?" is a testable card. "Explain immunity" is not.

Adding new cards faster than reviewing old ones. If you create 50 new cards per day and review 20, your review queue grows by 30 cards per day until the system is unmanageable. Pace new cards to a review load you can actually keep up with every day.

Quitting before the gaps get long. Spacing pays off over time, and the benefit shows up on delayed tests, not in the first few sessions. Judging the system after a week, before any long gaps have happened, means judging it before it has had a chance to work.

Spaced Repetition by Subject Type

Spaced repetition fits some kinds of material better than others. Knowing where it fits helps you build it into a broader study system rather than applying it to everything.

High fit: factual, discrete items. Vocabulary, definitions, dates, formulas, drug mechanisms, anatomical structures, foreign language words. This is also where most of the research was done: Cepeda et al.'s 2006 review covered verbal recall tasks only.

Medium fit: conceptual understanding. Spaced repetition can work for concepts if cards are written carefully to test application rather than definition. "What happens to cortisol when the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis is activated?" is better than "What is the HPA axis?"

Lower fit for flashcards: problem-solving skills. Math, physics problem-solving, and coding need practice with varied problems more than recall of specific facts. Spacing still applies, but the practice itself should be problem sets, ideally with problem types mixed together. Our guide to interleaving covers the research on mixing problem types.

The bottom line on spaced repetition

Spaced repetition is less a study technique than a scheduling principle, and it rests on research going back to Ebbinghaus in 1885. Spread your reviews out, make the gaps longer as your exam gets further away, and recall actively each time instead of re-reading. Software helps but is not required, and the exact intervals matter less than the habit of coming back to material after you have started to forget it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is spaced repetition?

Spaced repetition is a way of scheduling review so that you come back to material at increasing time intervals, based on how well you know it. Material you know well gets reviewed less often; material you struggle with comes back sooner. It is built on the spacing effect, the finding that spread-out practice produces better long-term retention than massed practice.

Does spaced repetition actually work for studying?

The underlying spacing effect is one of the best-supported findings in learning research. Cepeda et al. (2006) reviewed 317 experiments from 184 articles on verbal recall and found spaced practice beat massed practice, with only 12 of 271 comparisons showing no benefit or a reversal. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated distributed practice high utility for students. Most of that evidence comes from verbal material and short retention intervals, so the case is strongest for facts, vocabulary and definitions. See our overview of study techniques for how it fits with other methods.

How long before you see results from spaced repetition?

The benefit shows up on delayed tests, not in the first session. For memory tested within a minute, Cepeda et al. (2006) found very short gaps worked best; the advantage of longer gaps grows as the test gets further away. In Cepeda et al. (2008), tested 7 to 350 days after a review, the best gap produced 64% more recall than no gap. If your exam is weeks away, start spacing now rather than waiting to see whether it works.

What is the best spaced repetition interval schedule?

There is no single best schedule, because the best gap depends on how long you need to remember. Cepeda et al. (2008) found the optimal gap was about 20 to 40% of a one-week test delay but only about 5 to 10% of a one-year delay, in a study with trivia facts and one review session. A practical starting point is 1 day, then 3 days, then 1 week, then 2 weeks, then monthly, with intervals growing after each successful recall and resetting to 1 day when you forget. Apps like Anki adjust intervals from your rating after each review.

Can you do spaced repetition without Anki or special software?

Yes. You can implement a manual spaced repetition system using index cards and a simple box system with dividers for different intervals, or by marking flashcard stacks with dates for next review. A calendar or planner also works. The software automates the scheduling, but the underlying principle does not require any particular tool.
