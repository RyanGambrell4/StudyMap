Is a 3.3 GPA good?

A 3.3 GPA is a B+ average on the standard 4.0 scale. It meets any graduate program minimum of 3.0. It is also below the median at most ABA law schools. Here is what it means in practice and where you stand.

What a 3.3 GPA means

On the University of Chicago grading scale a B+ is worth 3.3, so a 3.3 GPA is a B+ average; UT Austin counts a B+ as 3.33. A 3.3 is not a perfect record (it reflects a mix of strong and solid work across courses), but it clears any stated minimum of 3.0.

Context matters too. Grading differs between schools and majors, so the most useful comparison is local: check whether your registrar or department publishes grade distributions.

Is a 3.3 GPA good for graduate school?

A 3.3 clears any 3.0 minimum. If a graduate program sets a minimum GPA, it says so on its admissions page. What that means in practice:

  • Master's programs: A 3.3 meets a 3.0 minimum. Combined with strong test scores and recommendation letters, it can be competitive at programs well above the minimum tier.
  • PhD programs: Departments set their own expectations. Research output, publications, and fit with faculty interests matter alongside GPA; check the department's admissions page.
  • Professional programs: Law, MBA, and medical school each have their own benchmarks. See the sections below for detailed context on each.

The honest summary: a 3.3 meets stated minimums of 3.0 but falls short of the median at programs whose admitted students report higher GPAs, where you will need compensating strengths.

Is a 3.3 GPA good for law school?

Law school admissions weight GPA and LSAT more heavily than almost any other graduate path. Here is how a 3.3 lands across the law school landscape:

  • T14 law schools: The 14 schools usually called the T14 (Harvard, Yale, Columbia, Chicago, NYU, etc.) have median GPAs from 3.88 to 3.99. A 3.3 is below the 25th percentile at every one of them (the lowest is 3.74 at Michigan Law). An extraordinary LSAT score can occasionally move a 3.3 through, but it is an uphill application at every T14 school, where LSAT medians run from 170 to 174.
  • The national picture: Across all 196 ABA-accredited law schools, the median of school medians is 3.67, and half of schools report a median between 3.50 and 3.81. A 3.3 is below the median at most of them, so it works best paired with an LSAT above the school's median.
  • Schools with lower medians: About a quarter of ABA schools report a median at or below 3.50. Those are the schools where a 3.3 sits closest to the middle of the admitted class.

Law school figures: ABA Standard 509 disclosures, 2025 entering class (abarequireddisclosures.org).

If law school is your goal and you have a 3.3, your LSAT score is the primary lever. A strong LSAT can partially compensate at mid-tier programs and makes you meaningfully more competitive across the board.

Is a 3.3 GPA good for MBA programs?

MBA admissions look at GPA as one of several factors, alongside GMAT/GRE scores, professional experience, essays, and recommendations. Each program publishes a class profile with the GPA of its entering class, so compare a 3.3 with those figures rather than with a rule of thumb.

If you are below a program's reported figure, a high test score, strong career progression, demonstrated leadership and a clear post-MBA plan are what the application leans on.

Is a 3.3 GPA good for jobs?

A 3.3 matters for a job only where the employer asks for GPA. If an employer screens on GPA, the cutoff is usually stated in the posting. Where GPA is not part of the application, what you built, what you can demonstrate, and how you perform in an interview carry the decision.

If a posting you are targeting lists a cutoff above 3.3, networking with people at that firm and coming in through a referral can get you a conversation.

Should you try to improve a 3.3 GPA?

Whether it is worth significant effort to raise a 3.3 depends on your specific goal:

  • If you are targeting selective MBA or MD programs: Compare your GPA with the average each program reports for its entering class. If a 3.5 would move you past it, the push is worth serious effort. For T14 law the gap is wider: medians there run from 3.88 to 3.99, so pair any GPA work with LSAT prep.
  • If you are targeting solid programs outside the very top tier: A 3.3 is already a strong foundation. Investing effort in your test scores (LSAT, GMAT, MCAT), work experience, and application narrative will likely move the needle more than chasing another 0.1 GPA points.
  • If you are still enrolled: Calculate the math first. Use the grade calculator to model what scores you need on remaining assignments in your current courses to hit specific grade targets. Then use the cumulative GPA calculator to see what those semester grades would do to your overall GPA. Effort is best directed at high-credit courses where you are currently below your target grade.

Moving from 3.3 to 3.5 takes sustained strong performance. As an example: a student with 90 completed credits at a 3.3 who earns a 3.9 over the next 30 credits reaches 3.45 (414 grade points over 120 credits); reaching 3.5 from 90 credits at 3.3 takes 36 credits of straight 4.0 work. The more credits you have completed, the slower the GPA moves, which is exactly why the grade calculator matters more than intuition.
