AP English Language Study Schedule

The 8-week plan for AP English Language and Composition. Builds from the rhetorical situation in Week 1 through full timed practice exams in Week 8, with a dedicated week for each essay type and a week for the multiple-choice section, which mixes reading questions on passages with writing questions on drafts.

Exam format (from the Fall 2024 CED): 3 hours 15 minutes in total. Section I is 45 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes, worth 45% of the score: 23-25 reading questions on passages and 20-22 writing questions on drafts, in five sets. Section II is 3 essays in 2 hours 15 minutes, including a 15-minute reading period, worth 55%: Synthesis, Rhetorical Analysis and Argument, each scored out of 6 points, with about 40 minutes recommended per essay. The 2027 exam is on Wednesday, May 12, 2027, in the morning session.

The 8-week schedule

WeekFocusWhat to actually do
Week 1
Mar 15		The rhetorical situationLearn the parts of the rhetorical situation as the CED names them: exigence, purpose, audience, context and message. Apply them to every passage you read, from editorials to speeches. Then practice naming rhetorical choices (diction, syntax, juxtaposition, parallelism, the order of ideas, the kinds of evidence a writer picks) and explaining what each one does for the writer's purpose. Each session: pick a short passage (one page), identify 3 choices, explain what each one does. Do not write full essays yet.
Week 2
Mar 22		Rhetorical Analysis essayLearn the structure: an introduction with a defensible thesis that analyzes the writer's rhetorical choices, body paragraphs that move from choice to effect on the audience to purpose, and a brief conclusion. The passage is nonfiction prose of about 600 to 800 words. Practice writing one complete body paragraph per day in 20 minutes. By the end of the week, write one full rhetorical analysis essay in 40 minutes on a released AP prompt and score it against the 6-point rubric.
Week 3
Mar 29		Synthesis essayThe synthesis question gives you six sources, two of them visual with at least one quantitative, and the text sources run about 500 words each. Practice reading and annotating all six in 15 minutes: the claim and one usable line or data point per source. Build a defensible position before writing. Your thesis should make an arguable claim, not summarize the sources. Use at least three sources, cited as Source A, Source B and so on, and explain how each one supports your reasoning. Write one timed synthesis essay at the end of the week (15 minutes reading plus 40 minutes writing).
Week 4
Apr 5		Argument essayThe prompt asks you to argue your position on a subject. Build a thesis with a clear claim and a line of reasoning, not just a topic statement. Use specific evidence of your own (a real event, a named person, a concrete example) rather than vague references, and write commentary that explains how each piece supports your claim. Practice qualifying your claim and, when it strengthens the argument, answering an opposing view. Write complete 40-minute timed argument essays every other day this week. Do not just outline; write to the end.
Week 5
Apr 12		Multiple-choice: reading and writing questionsReading sets: annotate the writer's claim, shifts in tone and the type of evidence on a fast first read, then answer questions on rhetorical situation, claims and evidence, reasoning and organization, and style. Writing sets: you read a draft and choose revisions, for example which sentence best supports a claim, where a sentence belongs, or which transition fits. Run one complete 45-question section under strict 60-minute timing. Sort every wrong answer by skill and by reading versus writing to find your pattern.
Week 6
Apr 19		Full free-response timed practiceWrite all 3 essays in a single 2-hour-15-minute sitting, including the 15-minute reading period, as the exam will run. Do not stop between essays. Use released AP prompts for each type: synthesis, rhetorical analysis, argument. Afterward, score every essay against the 6-point rubric (thesis, evidence and commentary, sophistication) and note where you lost points. This session tells you which essay type needs the most work in the final two weeks.
Week 7
Apr 26		Passage analysis drillingAnalyze one AP-style passage per day, Monday through Friday. For each passage, identify 3 distinct rhetorical choices and explain the purpose of each in a single structured sentence (choice, effect, purpose). Aim for about 10 minutes per passage. On Thursday, write one full rhetorical analysis essay under timed conditions. On Friday, redo the multiple-choice questions you missed in Week 5.
Week 8
May 3		Full practice exams & polishTake 2 complete practice exams (multiple-choice plus all 3 essays) with full timing. After each exam, review every wrong multiple-choice answer and decide whether it was a reading error, a timing error or a skill gap. Reread your essays and mark where a clearer thesis or stronger commentary would have earned more points. Monday and Tuesday of exam week: read one passage a day for fluency, then rest. The exam is Wednesday morning, May 12.

Score breakdown on the AP Lang exam

AP English Language is organized by skills, not content units. The CED has nine units but gives them no titles and no exam weights; teachers choose the themes. The exam is weighted by section, and the multiple-choice section is weighted by skill category.

  • Multiple Choice (Section I): 45% of the score. 45 questions in 60 minutes: two reading sets of 11-14 questions each and three writing sets (7-9, 7-9 and 4-6 questions).
  • Free Response (Section II): 55% of the score. 3 essays in 2 hours 15 minutes, including a 15-minute reading period.
  • Synthesis essay: 6 points. Develop your own position using at least three of six provided sources.
  • Rhetorical Analysis essay: 6 points. Analyze the rhetorical choices a writer makes in a nonfiction passage.
  • Argument essay: 6 points. Argue your position on a subject using evidence of your own.

Multiple-choice skill weights: Rhetorical Situation (reading 11-14%, writing 11-14%), Claims and Evidence (reading 13-16%, writing 11-14%), Reasoning and Organization (reading 13-16%, writing 11-14%) and Style (reading 11-14%, writing 11-14%). For quick review of the key terms, use the AP English Language flashcards, which follow the same framework.

Source: College Board, AP English Language and Composition Course and Exam Description, Effective Fall 2024 (exam format, multiple-choice skill weights, free-response question descriptions and scoring guidelines). 2027 exam date from the College Board's 2027 AP Exam Schedule.

What most AP English Language students get wrong

They write about what the passage says instead of how it says it. The rhetorical analysis prompt does not ask you to summarize the writer's argument or explain whether you agree. It asks you to analyze the rhetorical choices the writer makes to achieve a purpose. If your body paragraphs describe content rather than explain choices, you are answering the wrong question.

They treat the synthesis essay like a research report. Many students summarize each source in turn without making an arguable claim. The rubric gives the thesis point only for a defensible position, and the evidence points depend on using at least three sources to support your line of reasoning, not presenting them as equal viewpoints. You need a stance before you start writing, not after.

They chase the sophistication point with a single phrase. The rubric's sophistication point is not earned by "While some may argue..." or a sweeping opening like "Since the beginning of time." It goes to responses that explore complexities or tensions, explain the implications or limitations of an argument in a broader context, make rhetorical choices that consistently strengthen the argument, or use a consistently vivid and persuasive style. Answering an opposing view can help, but only if you actually work through it.

They forget that almost half the multiple-choice section is writing questions. 20-22 of the 45 questions ask you to revise a draft. Students who only practice passage-reading questions meet these for the first time on exam day. Include writing sets in every timed practice.

They run out of time on the multiple-choice section. Sixty minutes for 45 questions averages 80 seconds per question, and the reading passages require going back to the text. Practice the full section under real timing at least twice before the exam so you know your natural pace.

The 4-week compressed version

  • Week 1: The rhetorical situation and rhetorical choices, plus rhetorical analysis paragraph drills. Write one full rhetorical analysis essay by the end of the week.
  • Week 2: Synthesis essay (six-source reading, thesis, citing sources) and argument essay (claim, specific evidence, commentary). One timed essay of each type. One full multiple-choice section, reading and writing sets, under 60-minute timing.
  • Week 3: Complete timed free-response sitting (all 3 essays, 2 hours 15 minutes). Score with the rubric. Identify your weakest essay type and write 3 more timed essays of that type. One more full multiple-choice section.
  • Week 4: Two complete practice exams with full scoring and error analysis. One passage per day for fluency. Rest the day before the exam.
