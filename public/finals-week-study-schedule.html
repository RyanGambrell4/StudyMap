How the planner decides

There are four rules, and nothing else is going on behind them:

  1. Only finals that haven't happened yet get time. No course is ever scheduled on or after its exam date.
  2. Hours follow readiness gap and how soon the exam is. Each open final gets a weight of gap ÷ days until the exam, where the gap is 3 for lost, 2 for shaky and 1 for solid. A day's hours are split in proportion to those weights, in half-hour blocks.
  3. The day before each final is a practice test for that course. The planner sets aside one hour for it first (more if the weights give it more), so the last session before every exam is a timed practice test or a full retrieval session.
  4. Exam days are lighter. On a day you sit a final, the planner plans half your usual hours and gives them to the finals still ahead.

Here is rule 2 with numbers. Say it is Saturday, Organic Chemistry is on Tuesday (3 days away, shaky), Calculus II is on Thursday (5 days away, lost) and Intro Psychology is on Friday (6 days away, solid). The weights are 2 ÷ 3 = 0.67, 3 ÷ 5 = 0.60 and 1 ÷ 6 = 0.17. With 6 hours to spend, that works out to 3 hours, 2.5 hours and half an hour. As Tuesday gets closer, Organic Chemistry's weight climbs, and once it is over its hours move to the other two.

The planner can't know how much a final counts toward your grade. If a course you feel solid in still has a lot riding on the final, mark it shaky so it gets more time. The final exam calculator tells you the score you need on each one.

A finals-week method that fits the plan

1. Put every final in one list

Course, date, time, room, and what the final is worth. Then run each course through the final exam calculator so you know the score you need. That number, more than how scary the course feels, should decide your readiness marks. If you like paper, the printable finals planner has a table for exactly this.

2. Count the hours you actually have

Take out class, work, meals, travel and sleep, then enter what is left as your hours per day. A plan you can follow at 5 hours beats one you abandon at 10. If some days are busier than others, plan for the busy ones.

3. Spend the hours on recall, not rereading

Every study block in the plan is meant for retrieval practice: closing the notes and answering questions, working problems, or writing out what you remember and then checking it. In Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 study, students who read a passage once and then took three recall tests remembered 61% of the ideas a week later, against 40% for students who read it four times. Restudying looked better on a test given five minutes after studying, and the restudy group expected to remember more, which is why rereading feels like it is working. A 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing and spreading study over time as "high utility" techniques, and rereading, highlighting and summarizing as "low utility".

4. Mix problem types in math and science

For problem-solving courses, don't do twenty of one problem type and then twenty of the next. Shuffle them so you have to figure out which method each problem needs, which is what the exam asks of you. In a small study by Rohrer and Taylor (2007), students who practiced mixed math problems scored 63% on a test a week later, against 20% for students who practiced one type at a time, even though the blocked group did better during practice itself (89% vs 60%).

5. Make the last session a real practice test

The day before each final, sit an old exam or a set of questions you haven't seen, timed, with no notes. Then spend the rest of that block on every question you missed or guessed. This tells you what is still weak while there is time to fix the worst of it, and it gets you used to working at exam pace.

6. Keep sleep in the plan

Set a stop time each night and keep it. Late-night hours during finals week tend to be slow and error-prone, and you pay for them in the exam room the next morning. If the plan only works by cutting sleep, lower your hours per day and let the weights decide what gets less.

Questions

How does the finals week planner split my hours?

Each day, your hours go only to finals that have not happened yet. Every final gets a weight equal to its readiness gap (lost = 3, shaky = 2, solid = 1) divided by the number of days until the exam, and the hours are split in half-hour blocks in proportion to those weights. The day before each final, that course gets at least one hour for a timed practice test.

What if two finals are on the same day?

Both get a practice test the day before. The planner sets aside one hour for each before splitting the rest of that day's hours, so neither one loses its last session.

Should I study on the day of a final?

The planner never schedules study for a final on or after its exam date. On a day when you sit a final, it plans half your usual hours and gives them to the finals still ahead, since the exam itself takes most of that day.

Why does the exam I feel worst about get the most time?

Because it has the most room to improve. A course you already feel solid in gets a third of the weight of one you feel lost in on the same date. If the solid course still has a lot riding on the final, mark it shaky instead. The final exam calculator shows what score each final needs.
